\subsection{\src{Neural network: architecture and preprocessing}}\label{sec:network}

\src{As a second alternative, we fit a neural network. A feedforward neural network passes the inputs through layers of neurons, each of which applies a non-linear activation function, such as the sigmoid, to a weighted sum of its inputs. This non-linearity is what lets the network learn non-linear relationships (Wüthrich \& Merz, 2023, pp. 269–273). We use the R package \emph{nnet}, which fits networks with a single hidden layer and optimises the weights with the Broyden–Fletcher–Goldfarb–Shanno (BFGS) method (Ripley \& Venables, 2026; Venables \& Ripley, 2002). Since flexible networks can also fit the noise in the training data, we keep the network small and add weight decay, which can improve generalisation (Krogh \& Hertz, 1992, p. 950). The 13 inputs are rescaled to [0, 1], as inputs on similar scales help the weights learn at a similar rate (LeCun et al., 1998, Sect. 4.3). The minimum and maximum come from the training set only, so that no information from the test set leaks into the model (Kapoor \& Narayanan, 2023, pp. 1–3).}

\subsection{\src{Neural network: tuning and threshold selection}}\label{sec:network-tuning}

\src{We tune the number of hidden neurons (1, 2, 3 or 5) and the weight decay (0.01, 0.1, 0.5 or 1) with the \emph{caret} package (Kuhn, 2008). As for the random forest, we use country-grouped 10-fold CV (Section~\ref{sec:data-sources}). Models are compared by balanced accuracy (Section~\ref{sec:prediction-task}).}

\src{The best network has two hidden neurons and a decay of 0.01, with a cross-validated balanced accuracy of 0.650. With the same light decay, five neurons only reach 0.544, while a strong decay of 1 keeps every size between 0.510 and 0.530, close to the 0.500 of a model that always predicts the same class. With 145 training programmes, a small and lightly regularised network therefore works best. We then choose the classification threshold that maximises balanced accuracy on the out-of-fold predictions, so that the test set plays no role in model selection. This gives a threshold of 0.44 and an out-of-fold balanced accuracy of 0.684.}

\src{At their selected thresholds, the network beats the random forest on balanced accuracy (0.556 against 0.508) but not on AUC (0.567 against 0.586), and both reach an accuracy of only 0.500, below the 0.607 of predicting an interruption for every programme. At a common threshold of 0.50, the three models reach balanced accuracies of 0.567 (forest), 0.570 (logistic regression) and 0.572 (network), within 0.005 of each other. The two models also make different errors: at their selected thresholds of 0.44 and 0.51, respectively, the network rarely predicts an interruption (sensitivity 0.294, specificity 0.818), while the forest is more balanced (0.471 and 0.545). The network’s result is also fragile. Refitting it with 20 different random seeds gives test balanced accuracies between 0.436 and 0.559 (median 0.553), so the 0.556 in Table~\ref{tab:model-comparison} sits near the top of a range that reaches down to 0.436. This is expected, since the starting weights are drawn randomly (Wüthrich \& Merz, 2023, p. 284) and the fitted network is not unique.}

\src{We thus find no reliable winner between the two models. The network’s balanced accuracy also falls from 0.684 out of fold, which is optimistic because the settings and the threshold were chosen on those predictions (cross-validated 0.650), to 0.556 on the later test set. Since it brings no clear accuracy gain over the other models, we would not use the neural network to flag programmes at risk of interruption.}
